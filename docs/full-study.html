Beyond Keywords

Making Legal Document Search Measurably Better

Independent, hypothetical product case study | AI Search, relevance evaluation, experimentation, product judgment

Simulation disclosure. This is an independent concept project inspired by a publicly described legal search product challenge. It is not a Clio project and does not represent access to Clio customers, telemetry, architecture, staff, or product plans. Every user scenario, dataset, metric, result, team constraint, timeline, and iteration below is assumed or simulated. Numbers demonstrate how I would reason and make decisions; they are not observed outcomes or evidence that a product shipped.

The decision in one minute

Assumed user job. A legal professional wants to locate the correct, authorized, up-to-date source quickly—even when they remember the meaning of a document rather than its exact title.

Product question. When is AI-based semantic retrieval worth introducing into legal search, and how would I know that it helps instead of merely producing more plausible-looking matches?

Working hypothesis. Lexical search is strong for filenames, matter identifiers, and exact citations, but can miss clauses expressed in different language. Semantic retrieval could close part of that gap. It cannot fix absent documents, invalid access controls, incorrect matter associations, or ambiguous legal status.

Proposed decision. Evaluate lexical improvements first, then selectively add hybrid lexical–semantic retrieval for query segments where semantic mismatch is the demonstrated bottleneck. Keep exact matching, permissions, verified metadata, latency, and cost as release guardrails.

What this case demonstrates. AI suitability judgment → explicit quality standard → simulated failed experiment → diagnosis and iteration → evidence-gated product decision.

1. Start from the task, not from the model

Assumed scenario A: known document. A lawyer types Acme NDA 2025 and expects the correct file near the top. Query rewriting or semantic similarity could make this query worse by prioritizing thematically related but incorrect agreements.

Assumed scenario B: remembered meaning. A lawyer types the agreement allowing termination without a penalty. The relevant passage may instead say may withdraw without an early exit fee. Literal term overlap is weak, yet the legal professional needs the original clause, not an AI summary.

Assumed scenario C: missing source. A lawyer searches for the email approving the revised schedule, but the relevant email was never indexed. No embedding model can retrieve absent content.

The complaint “I cannot find my document” therefore branches into different potential failures:

Assumed discovery protocol. Under appropriate privacy controls, I would examine de-identified failed-query samples, label common intents, review support themes, and observe representative legal search tasks. I would not infer that vocabulary mismatch dominates until those sources agreed.

Decision gate 1. If most high-value failures are caused by missing source coverage or invalid metadata, defer semantic work and redirect capacity to content quality. AI is justified only if meaning mismatch accounts for a meaningful and addressable portion of failures.

2. Why AI here—and why not everywhere?

I would compare three product options rather than assuming the newest model wins.

OptionWhy it might workWhere it breaksInitial choice
Improve lexical / structured searchFast, explainable and precise for names, numbers, citations and filtersCannot consistently connect paraphrased meaningsKeep as essential baseline
Semantic-only embeddingsCan retrieve conceptually related text with low word overlapMay confuse similar agreements, entities, exceptions or legal termsReject as universal replacement
Hybrid retrieval with optional rerankingCombines exact evidence and conceptual recallRaises operational complexity, cost and possible latencyTest selectively, not unconditionally

AI suitability test. I would use semantic retrieval when the user's information need depends on meaning beyond lexical overlap, the source material exists and is authorized, and a labeled evaluation demonstrates improvement. I would not use an LLM to guess whether a contract was signed, invent document metadata, grant permissions, or substitute an answer for an authoritative source.

Proposed flow (conceptual, not an existing system):

Authorized query scope → query / intent analysis → lexical and/or vector candidate retrieval → permission-constrained fusion → optional reranking → source-backed results and preview

Access restrictions must be applied early enough to prevent protected material leaking into candidates, logging, caches, snippets or AI contexts, and rechecked at delivery. Query classification itself can start with deterministic rules; an ML classifier is warranted only if it improves quality enough to justify maintenance.

Explicit non-goal for the first release: generative answer cards. Finding and verifying the canonical source is the primary job. Generated prose would introduce additional hallucination, attribution and evaluation burdens before retrieval is trustworthy.

3. Define “good” before running an experiment

The north-star outcome would be Verified Search Task Success: in a controlled task, can the user correctly identify and open the requested authorized source, with the necessary matter/version evidence, within an acceptable effort budget?

This cannot be inferred from click-through rate alone: many clicks can signal confusion, and zero clicks can sometimes mean the snippet already solved the task. Behavioral metrics are diagnostics until validated against observed task completion.

Assumed offline evaluation design

  • Corpus: a synthetic, permission-labeled collection of 3,000 legal-style documents and communications, including revisions, near-duplicate contracts, matter IDs, signed/draft metadata and paraphrased clauses. The corpus would be fictional; no real client data.
  • Query set: 240 simulated queries: 60 known-item, 70 semantic, 45 contextual, 35 metadata-based and 30 difficult / ambiguous. Segment mix is a testing design, not a claim about production traffic.
  • Relevance judgments: two hypothetical reviewers use 0 = irrelevant, 1 = tangential, 2 = relevant, 3 = target/decisive. For selected tasks, they also mark the must-find source and whether the result is authorized. Disagreements are adjudicated; inter-rater agreement and judgment coverage are reported.
  • Data separation: a development set tunes fusion and query policies; a held-out set supports go/no-go decisions. Deduplicate close paraphrases across splits to avoid leakage.
  • Metrics: Success@1 and MRR for known-item queries; NDCG@10 and Recall@10 for semantic/contextual tasks; constraint precision for metadata tasks; verified task success in moderated exercises.
  • Guardrails: p95 latency, cost per 1,000 queries, index freshness, exact-ID regressions and negative authorization tests. For security, any confirmed unauthorized exposure is a hard stop, not an averageable metric.

Illustrative release thresholds (assumed, not company targets). Relative NDCG@10 improvement ≥10% on semantic queries; known-item Success@1 decline ≤1 percentage point; p95 latency ≤650 ms for the proposed route; per-query cost ≤2× baseline; and zero failed critical ACL tests. Actual thresholds would require baseline measurement, user tolerance testing and system cost analysis.

What would count as evidence?

Offline retrieval labels tell us whether relevance improved on the synthetic benchmark. They do not establish that lawyers complete real tasks faster. After offline gates, I would run a privacy-reviewed shadow test and, if safe, a limited randomized rollout with prespecified exposure rules, power assumptions, task-success validation and rollback conditions.

The temptation: ship v1 because semantic NDCG improves by roughly 22% relative to baseline.

My decision: do not ship. A large semantic uplift does not compensate for known-item regression or latency beyond the precommitted gate. The success of one segment cannot hide harm to another.

Failure analysis: what went wrong?

In this imagined error review, 12 of 60 known-item queries lose their correct first result. Hypothetically, the pattern is concentrated in document identifiers and near-duplicate agreements. Three failure hypotheses emerge:

  1. Fusion imbalance: vector matches can outrank exact identifier hits.
  2. Query routing weakness: short identifier queries are treated as semantic questions.
  3. Reranking expense: a second-stage model adds latency without clear benefit to exact queries.

I would separate these hypotheses with query-level traces and targeted ablations, not attribute the failure to embeddings without evidence. I would also inspect metadata constraint errors independently: a ranking fix cannot correct a wrongly parsed date filter.

5. Simulated iteration 2: improve by narrowing where AI is used

Proposed changes, still hypothetical:

  • Add deterministic detection and lexical-first routing for high-confidence matter IDs, exact filenames and structured citation patterns.
  • Cap semantic influence or apply exact-match boosts when a trustworthy identifier is present.
  • Require validated structured filters to remain hard constraints; do not let semantic similarity override signed status, dates or authorization.
  • Use reranking only for complex exploratory queries where an offline ablation shows incremental value.
  • Cache safe reusable computation and set route-specific latency budgets; do not cache sensitive results outside their authorization context.
  • Improve result cards with grounded matched passages, source matter and explicitly known version metadata. No generated explanation may assert unsupported legal status.

6. Experience design: retrieve, inspect, verify

Simulated user query: Find the latest signed NDA with Acme.

A proposed interface displays the parsed concepts Acme, NDA, Signed, Most recent as editable interpretations. If signing status is unknown, the system must say unknown—not infer signed from a filename containing “final.”

Each result provides its canonical title and source, matter association where authoritative, a verifiable snippet, and available version/date fields. A preview shows the actual matched passage and links back to the source. If no authorized precise result exists, the UI distinguishes approximate matches from verified matches.

How I would evaluate UX quality: moderated simulated tasks measure correct-source selection, verification time, false-confidence errors and participants' ability to notice ambiguous versions. A visually polished result card is not a success if it causes a lawyer to choose the wrong agreement faster.

7. Execution plan and decision ownership

Assumed delivery setting: one PM, one designer, five search engineers, two ML engineers and security support; a 12-week planning window. These are scenario constraints, not Clio staffing or commitments.

Simulated stageWorkDecision gate
Weeks 1–3Map failures; approve synthetic labels and trace instrumentationAI-relevant failure mode materially present?
Weeks 4–6Compare lexical improvements, hybrid v1 and ablationsSegment uplift without critical regression?
Weeks 7–9Iterate routing, ranking, UX and safety testsHeld-out and operational gates passed?
Weeks 10–12Shadow test, limited controlled exposure, review user tasksEvidence sufficient to expand or rollback?

I would own the definition of user value, prioritization, acceptance thresholds and documented trade-offs. ML and search engineers would challenge technical assumptions and own implementation quality; design would test verification usability; security/privacy would validate data handling; launch decisions would be joint and gate-based.

A weekly failure review would inspect worst-query segments, top regressions, permission tests, latency distribution and unexpected query mixes. Every iteration would preserve a baseline and a written hypothesis, so a quality change can be attributed to a specific product or retrieval decision.

Decision after the simulated v2

Conditional recommendation: pursue a narrow rollout of intent-aware hybrid search only after a new held-out dataset, access-control checks, shadow tests and real task validation. Do not claim an achieved improvement or ship based solely on the illustrative table above.

What I learned from the simulated trade-off

More AI was not the answer to every search failure. The strongest product judgment was to keep exact retrieval and hard constraints intact, use semantic understanding where paraphrases genuinely created misses, and require measured evidence before broadening exposure.

The measure of an intelligent search product is not how often it uses AI; it is how reliably people find and verify the correct source.

One Search, Every Matter

Building a Shared, Permission-Aware Search Platform Across Legal Workflows

Independent, hypothetical product case study | Platform strategy, AI suitability, cross-product quality, failure-driven iteration

Simulation disclosure. This is an independent concept exploration inspired by a legal software suite with multiple products and AI consumers. It does not describe Clio’s internal architecture, customer data, team capacity, releases, incidents, or results. Every workflow, source contract, dataset, milestone, metric, numerical outcome, and failure below is assumed or simulated. It illustrates decisions to test, not work completed.

The decision in one minute

Assumed user job. A lawyer wants to find the original authorized evidence behind a matter-level question, regardless of whether the source is a document, communication or record.

Platform question. How should a search team increase information coverage across products without making relevance, permissions and provenance worse—and which parts actually need AI?

Working hypothesis. Some search failures are caused by product fragmentation and missing content, not by insufficient semantic understanding. A shared ingestion and authorization foundation should therefore precede indiscriminate AI-based federation. AI is useful when a cross-source query requires language understanding or passage retrieval; it is not a substitute for source integrations, identity mapping or policy enforcement.

Proposed decision. Prove one complete, permission-safe cross-source task first, with explicit content contracts, source-aware evaluation and a retrieval profile appropriate to that task. Expand only when coverage, relevance, freshness and safety all pass evidence gates.

What this case demonstrates. System-level problem diagnosis → build-versus-AI judgment → cross-product quality evaluation → simulated platform failure and repair → constrained roadmap and leadership decisions.

1. Distinguish the source problem from the retrieval problem

Simulated lawyer task: Where did the client first approve the revised payment schedule?

In this fictional matter, relevant evidence exists in an intake note, a later email and a revised contract. A document-only index retrieves the contract, but not the original approval. From the user’s perspective the answer is incomplete; from a semantic ranker’s perspective the indexed contract may appear highly relevant.

Potential failure causes should be diagnosed separately:

Failure classExample in simulated taskSuitable response
CoverageApproval email absent from indexSource integration; AI cannot retrieve absent content

AuthorizationEmail is visible to the wrong userPolicy and ACL correction; hard release stop
FreshnessEdited note still appears in old formEvent processing, invalidation and SLOs
RelevanceCorrect authorized email indexed but buriedLexical / semantic hybrid ranking experiment
Evidence completenessAI consumer retrieves one of three needed sourcesEvidence-focused retrieval and coverage evaluation

Key strategic insight: “Unified search” is not synonymous with “apply embeddings to everything.” It is a data, product, trust and evaluation problem with a selective AI component.

2. Where AI belongs in a shared platform

I would separate deterministic platform obligations from probabilistic retrieval intelligence.

Deterministic foundation (AI is not the right tool): record identity; tenant and matter authorization; create/update/delete events; source ownership; canonical URLs; known document status; processing-purpose restrictions; audit logs. These are correctness contracts, not predictions.

Potential AI contribution: semantic matching for descriptions that span email, note and contract language; passage-level candidate discovery; reranking among already authorized candidates; evidence retrieval profiles for downstream AI analysis.

What I would refuse to infer: an unknown matter relationship, a missing signature, authority to view a record, or proof that an AI answer is legally correct. Such fields require authoritative data and explicit policy.

Proposed shared architecture (conceptual)

Product source + authoritative identity/policy → source adapter / change stream → normalized search record → permission-aware full-text and vector indexes → retrieval profiles → Search API → Global / Matter / AI consumers

Permission checks must cover every exposure channel and be revisited at read time where stale index metadata can create risk. AI customer preferences must be mapped to specific processing purposes; search indexing, model inference and model training are not automatically equivalent.

A deliberately limited first slice

The assumed minimum product is not one search box across the full suite. It is a matter-scoped search task covering an existing document source plus one communications source, with canonical links, reliable permissions, clear source labels and a measurable definition of completeness. New product sources would be considered only after this vertical slice is trustworthy.

3. Specify contracts before promising universal search

A conceptual normalized record contains:

  • Source product, canonical source ID, content type and immutable record identity.
  • Explicit matter associations, when known; unknown association remains unknown.
  • Searchable title/body and passage offsets with provenance.
  • Authoritative timestamps, optional status/version fields, and indexing timestamp.
  • ACL reference, tenant boundary, purpose-specific processing policy and canonical source link.
  • Update/delete event identifiers for replay, deduplication and reconciliation.

The proposed API returns authorized ranked result IDs, grounded snippets, source labels, matter context, freshness indicators and pagination. Consumer profiles can change ranking objectives, not access rights.

Platform ownership: ingestion contracts, index health, authorization plumbing, core retrieval, relevance tooling and APIs. Consumer team ownership: workflow presentation, filters, task-specific quality and rollout. Source teams remain accountable for the truth of their original data and events.

This boundary avoids two opposite failures: every product rebuilding its own retriever, or one central team imposing an inappropriate universal result experience.

4. Evaluate cross-product quality as an end-to-end task

A platform can have excellent NDCG on indexed documents and still fail users if the required email is absent. Accordingly, quality must include coverage, correctness, freshness, relevance and task completion.

Assumed benchmark

Construct 120 synthetic matter-level tasks over fictional documents, emails and intake records. For each task, label (a) all required source records, (b) their provenance and matter relationship, (c) authorization by test persona, and (d) whether the user can reach a defensible conclusion from the retrieved evidence. Use independently adjudicated labels and a held-out task set; report performance by source type and consumer profile.

Quality dimensionMetric / testWhy it matters
Content coverageRequired-source coverageIs the evidence indexed at all?
RelevanceSource-aware Recall@10 and NDCG@10Is the right evidence retrieved and ranked?
CompletenessAll-required-evidence@10Are multiple necessary sources present together?
CorrectnessMatter/source identity accuracyIs content associated with the right matter?
FreshnessUpdate / deletion / revocation propagationCan users rely on current information?
TrustCross-tenant, ACL and snippet negative testsCould protected content leak?
Human outcomeVerified cross-source task successCan users find the original correct evidence?
AI consumer (if added)Citation support / evidence coverageAre downstream claims grounded in authorized sources?

Illustrative gates, not real service commitments: ≥90% required-source coverage for the chosen scope; ≥85% all-required-evidence@10 on the held-out tasks; ≥90% matter association accuracy; ≥95% updates visible within an assumed freshness objective; no critical permission or deletion-test failures. Each threshold should be negotiated based on task criticality and baseline, and all should be segmented by source type.

Interpretation rule: retrieval quality is meaningful only conditional on eligible and authorized source coverage. I would report missing-source failures separately rather than excluding them from the headline task-success result.

My decision: stop rollout, even though coverage improves. The simulated critical ACL failure is sufficient on its own. Moreover, retrieval quality, metadata correctness and freshness degrade. Counting more indexed records would be a misleading success claim.

Simulated failure review

Hypothetical traces reveal four failure patterns:

  1. Incorrect matter joins: matching contact names creates false associations across matters.
  2. Source-score mismatch: raw lexical scores from email and document indexes are merged without calibration; short emails swamp long documents.
  3. Stale access policy: an ACL change arrives after the content update, leaving a transient result exposure.
  4. Incomplete lifecycle handling: edited emails remain searchable during delayed change-event processing.

These are distinct root causes. Adding a stronger embedding model could not fix identity joins, missing revocations or event lag; it could even hide them under more plausible search results.

6. Simulated iteration 2: fix correctness, then selectively apply AI

Proposed remediation (hypothetical):

  • Require authoritative source IDs and matter relationships; never guess links from names or semantic similarity.
  • Enforce identity-scoped access at query and result time, invalidate affected caches on revocation, and fail closed if permissions are uncertain.
  • Add replay-safe source events, deletion tombstones, freshness alerts and reconciliation against source-of-truth counts.
  • Normalize per-source retrieval candidates with evaluated fusion instead of comparing incomparable raw scores.
  • Test semantic passage retrieval only after content coverage and authorization are reliable; use it for paraphrased approval language, not metadata correctness.
  • Return the original email/document with source context rather than generating a synthetic “client approved” assertion.

AI quality for downstream consumers

If an AI assistant later consumes this layer, its evaluation must be separate from human search. In a hypothetical task requiring three pieces of evidence, retrieval of only one piece is insufficient even if a generated answer sounds coherent. I would track required-evidence coverage, citation-to-passage support and unsupported assertion rate. I would not treat LLM fluency or user thumbs-up as proof of factual grounding.

7. Prioritize under a constrained platform budget

Assumed organization: one shared search squad (one PM, six engineers and two ML engineers), part-time source product owners, security/privacy partners and a two-quarter horizon. This is an exercise in prioritization, not a statement about Clio.

Simulated two-quarter sequence

Quarter 1 — Prove one end-to-end job. Agree on source contract and ownership; instrument source coverage; test ACL and deletion flows; build one document-plus-communication journey; benchmark required-evidence completeness; resolve failures before a pilot.

Quarter 2 — Expand conditionally. If the first slice meets trust, freshness, relevance and task-success gates, add one additional source or consumer profile. If the limiting factor is a broken source event stream or unstable matter mapping, fund that repair instead of new integrations.

Explicit pivot trigger: If the assumed first-quarter diagnosis shows that >50% of priority misses originate in poor within-source ranking rather than absent communications, swap the first cross-source milestone for a search relevance initiative. This threshold is a hypothetical decision aid, not a discovered fact.

Team leadership and accountability

The PM owns user-job prioritization, business trade-offs, explicit gates and the sequencing proposal; source PMs own source-product readiness and requirements; engineering owns ingestion/index resilience; ML owns retrieval experiments and ranking analysis; security/privacy owns policy review and critical negative-test sign-off; design owns verification workflows.

A shared decision log would record the user task, suspected cause, alternative considered, evidence required, owner, gate and rollback trigger. Weekly dependency reviews and end-to-end demos help expose integration risks before they become missed milestones.

8. What success would—and would not—mean

Success would mean an actual future experiment shows that authorized users find the right cross-source evidence more often and with less effort, while partner products consume a reliable shared service. Measures would include verified task success, all-required-evidence coverage, source/index freshness, API reliability, onboarding effort and security checks.

It would not mean simply that the search index holds more records, a diagram shows more arrows, or an AI assistant produces a fluent summary. The platform must make trustworthy evidence available, not merely create the appearance of connected intelligence.

Unresolved questions to validate: Which cross-source jobs are frequent and valuable? How trustworthy are matter links? Which sources are eligible under actual policies? What freshness budgets do different consumers need? Where does semantic retrieval add measurable value after deterministic correctness is secured?

A shared AI Search platform earns the right to scale by proving that it can retrieve the right evidence, for the right user, from the right source—and recover safely when it fails.

Both studies are independent, simulated thought experiments. No internal company information, user research findings, experimental outcomes, product launches, or business results are claimed.
